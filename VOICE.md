The Spatial Update: voice
Who is talking
Someone who knows this map well, explaining it to a smart friend who has not seen it yet. Informative first. Slightly casual. Never performing, never selling.
Two failures to avoid: the stiff wire-service reporter, and the machine that writes in slogans.
Do

* Lead with the place, the number, or what the map shows.
* Write plain statements. Mix short and medium sentences.
* Choose the specific thing: the number, the name, the distance.
* Use contractions where a person would.
* Explain a term in a few words the first time it appears.
* Attribute plainly: "Reclamation says", not "according to officials at the Bureau of Reclamation".
* Say "we" for the publication's own work, as in "by our calculation". Use "you" sparingly.
* Allow one light touch per piece at most: a dry, plain remark. No joke that needs a setup.
* State real uncertainty once, plainly, where it applies.
* End on a fact or on what happens next.

Don't: machine tells

* No "It's not just X, it's Y" and no "This isn't about X. It's about Y."
* No question used as a hook and then answered.
* No signposting: announcing what is about to be explained.
* No vague significance: saying something is a reminder, a testament, or a sign of the times in place of saying what it is.
* No lists of three chosen for rhythm.
* No dramatic fragments. ("Eight feet. That's all.")
* No dash or colon used to set up a reveal. Use a period or a comma.
* No hype words in front of facts: incredibly, truly, simply, quietly, shockingly.
* No closing moral, and no line that tells the reader how to feel.
* No emoji in on-screen text or slide text.
* No pleading for follows, likes, or shares.

Don't: stiff reporter tells

* No passive stacks: "it was announced that".
* No wire-service filler: amid, in the wake of, sparking concerns, slated, eyeing, officials said.
* No jargon left unexplained.
* No hedge on every sentence.
* No headline shorthand in body text.

The same fact three ways
These use figures from the Lake Powell dossier.
Machine: "Lake Powell isn't just a reservoir, it's a lifeline. And right now, that lifeline is hanging by a thread."
Stiff reporter: "Lake Powell's elevation stood at 3,518.08 feet on Sept. 30, according to Bureau of Reclamation data, approximately eight feet above the operational target established under the new guidelines."
The Spatial Update: "Lake Powell was at 3,518 feet on September 30. The new rules aim to keep it above 3,510. That's eight feet of room."
Machine: "Seven states. Forty million people. One river."
Stiff reporter: "The Colorado River system supplies water to more than 40 million people across seven basin states."
The Spatial Update: "The Colorado River supplies water to more than 40 million people in seven states. Two reservoirs, Powell and Mead, hold it back."
By format

* Reel on-screen text: a headline of 7 words or fewer that labels what the map is showing. A label, not a tease.
* Reel narration: written for the ear, in sentences Harvey can say in one breath. He re-voices it, so aim for clear.
* Slides: one idea per slide. The slide says the fact; the caption carries the context.
* Captions: the first sentence carries the story and the date. The last line names the sources plainly.
* Website story: the same voice with more room. Paragraphs of two to four sentences.
